Two facts the whole report leans on, both from the code rather than the slides:

- The band is `K × max(weighted MAD, SIGMA_MIN)` with `K = 1.25`, `SIGMA_MIN = 0.05`, so the narrowest half-band is 0.0625 and a total forfeit begins 0.125 from the centre (`TOTAL_FORFEIT_BANDS = 2`). The published anchors sit 0.25 apart.

## 1. My best forecast of this room is my model of this room, not my reading

**From the floor.** "You say my best forecast of the hidden centre is my own careful reading. But I know who is in this room: thirty open-science people, mostly life and social sciences, who dislike PDF and have never opened LaTeX. My best forecast of where *they* land is my model of them, and your own notes say the mean 'reflects the whole room'. Why would I score the evidence when you pay me to score the room?"

**Angle.** Epistemic.

**Target.** Slide 6, "Why hidden, and why honest": "With nothing to copy, your best forecast of where careful strangers land is your own careful reading of the evidence against the prompt. The prompt is the Schelling point: the answer strangers converge on without talking, because each expects the others to read it the same way. Any other strategy means guessing how the others will deviate, and the band makes a wrong guess expensive." Handout, "What the design is meant to do": "That is the bet: paying for closeness to an unseen centre pays for honest reading."

**Why it bites.** The argument assumes jurors know nothing about each other beyond the prompt. They are not strangers: they are a named roster on every phone (`seatedBlock(true, false)` renders every juror's name and running balance during scoring), in a room filtered by a conference theme. The materials concede that the centre is a function of who is in the room, not of the evidence: LaTeX note, "Your field is not the field ... Jurors who score their own desk scatter, and the band is as wide as the larger group's agreement, so the scattered ones pay"; NumPy note, "R users may land lower than Python users: the field effect again, and the mean reflects the whole room"; Linux note, "Coherence is measured against the room"; the phone itself, "Graded against the jury, not against the truth." If the centre reflects the room's composition, the payoff rewards predicting that composition. The prompt tells jurors to keep "Your own field or taste" separate; the payoff pays them to score the room's field and taste. The two instructions point in opposite directions and only one of them is backed by money.

**Strongest form.** This is Keynes's beauty contest (General Theory, 1936, ch. 12): the prize goes not to the face you find prettiest but to the one you expect others to pick, and so on to higher orders. Nagel (1995, American Economic Review, "Unraveling in guessing games") showed experimentally that people do reason about others' reasoning rather than report their own view when paid for closeness to an aggregate. Schelling's focal point (The Strategy of Conflict, 1960) is defined by salience, not by truth: the focal answer for Linux is "famous, therefore high", for arXiv "flooded with AI papers" (the bloc's cover story works precisely because it is salient), for PDF "where data goes to die". The deck's own Schelling definition, "each expects the others to read it the same way", is satisfied by a shared prejudice as well as by a shared reading. The Condorcet jury theorem and the wisdom-of-crowds results (Galton 1907; Page 2007, the diversity prediction theorem) require independent *errors*; sealing makes the reports independent, not the errors. Lorenz, Rauhut, Schweitzer and Helbing (2011, PNAS, "How social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect") showed that anything that pulls estimates toward the group's expected centre destroys the diversity that makes the aggregate accurate; paying for closeness to the centre is such a pull, applied before the first score is sealed.

**Trap.** Answering with slide 6's own line, "the band makes a wrong guess expensive." It is circular: the guess about the room is exactly what is paid. Also a trap: "we will make the prompt more concrete." A sharper prompt does not remove a bias the whole room shares. And "a shared prejudice is still a shared standard" concedes the attack: it admits the design pays for prejudice when prejudice is what the room shares.

## 2. The band wipes out the juror who knows better than the room

**From the floor.** "I run an HPC centre. I know most research in this building never touches a cluster; I scored Linux 0.75, your own anchor for 'widely relied on'. The room, which just heard 'runs every machine on the TOP500', scored 0.92, and I lost my whole stake for knowing more than they do. What exactly did you pay for?"

**Angle.** Epistemic.

**Target.** Slide 6, "jurors who know the field or recuse"; the Linux note shown on every phone after grading: "a juror at 0.75 among a jury at 0.92 can forfeit while being, in any ordinary sense, right."

**Why it bites.** The ledger does what the note says. Twenty-nine jurors spread 0.88 to 0.96 and one expert at 0.75: centre 0.915, half-band 0.063 (the floor), the expert is 2.63 bands out and forfeits 100% of the stake. At 0.80 the forfeit is 86%. The narrowest band (0.0625) is a quarter of the gap between the published anchors (0.25), so a juror who applies the anchor text literally can be wiped out by a room that applied the next anchor up. The design condition "jurors who know the field" is not merely unrewarded; the more a juror knows relative to the room, the more it costs. The materials admit this in a note the room reads after losing the stake, and offer nothing else: there is no appeal in the game, no reason field, no minority report. Slide 11's remedy, "Recusal lets a juror step out rather than invent a score", does not apply: the expert did not invent a score.

**Strongest form.** A funder wants the maximal competence in the room, not the modal one. Burgman et al. (2011, PLOS ONE, "Expert status and performance") found that self-assessed and peer-assessed expertise barely predicts accuracy; Cooke's classical model (Experts in Uncertainty, 1991) weights experts by demonstrated calibration on seed questions, because unweighted or consensus-weighted aggregates systematically lose the well-calibrated minority. The surprisingly-popular result (Prelec, Seo and McCoy, 2017, Nature) is the formal statement that when a minority knows better, the majority answer is wrong and the fix is to reward the answer that is *more common than the crowd predicts*, i.e. to reward exactly the juror this mechanism fines. Information cascades (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, 1992) are usually described as copying; this mechanism produces the same outcome without copying, by pricing dissent at up to the whole stake.

**Trap.** "That is the price of coherence; recuse if you disagree." That tells the expert to leave, which is attack 9. Also a trap: "we could widen the band or raise the floor." A wider band makes the bloc attack cheaper (attack 4) and pays less for care. And "they could appeal": the game has no appeal, and the proposed appeal goes to a larger jury drawn from the same room (attack 19).

## 3. Stake-weighted centre and band: whoever has the stake can never forfeit

**From the floor.** "The centre is the mean weighted by stake and the band follows the stake-weighted spread. So a juror with half the stake sets the centre and the yardstick, and can never be outside their own band. 'Concentrated stake remains a concern' is a polite way of saying the rich juror cannot lose. Is stake a token? Who issues it? Why should a library trust an importance score set by whoever bought the most of it?"

**Angle.** Political and legitimacy, with a crypto-scepticism edge.

**Target.** Slide 6, "The centre is the mean weighted by stake"; slide 12, "In the proposed design, stake weighs seats ... Concentrated stake remains a concern. This workshop uses equal seats and points, so it does not demonstrate that defence." Slide 4, "No account. No wallet."

**Why it bites.** `stakeWeightedMean` and `weightedMedian(entries, ...)` in `core.js` both weight by `e.stake`. Run on the ledger: one juror with 10 units of stake at 0.00 among fourteen at 0.90 moves the centre from 0.90 to 0.525 and forfeits 6% of their stake (1.06 bands out: by moving the centre the whale also moved every honest juror's distance, and the band is the median of those distances). With 51% of the stake the same juror forfeits nothing and takes 60% of the pot, and this is not luck: a juror holding more than half the stake has their own distance as the weighted median, and since `K = 1.25 > 1` the band always reaches past them. Such a juror cannot be outside the band whatever they score. The mean has a breakdown point of zero (one unbounded weight moves it anywhere) and the band is built from the same weights, so stake weighting removes the only defence the deck offers against a bloc, the forfeit, for exactly the actor the deck says it is worried about. The materials never say what stake is, who issues it, whether it is money, or how an open-science practitioner acquires it. The slide-4 reassurance "No wallet" is about the workshop; the proposed design is a token-weighted vote on what matters in research, which is the thing this audience came to be sceptical of.

**Strongest form.** Robust statistics: the breakdown point of the mean is 0, of the median 50% (Hampel 1971; Huber 1981); choosing the mean for the centre while using the median for the spread is choosing the fragile statistic for the quantity that matters. Token-weighted governance has a documented record: token-curated registries (Goldin 2017) and their critiques, the concentration of voting power in DeFi governance (Barbereau et al. 2022, "DeFi, not so decentralized"), and the Kleros/Augur discussions of 51% attacks on small courts. The audience's political form is Ostrom (Governing the Commons, 1990): a commons is defined by the rules its users set for themselves; a stake-weighted jury hands that rule-setting to capital.

**Trap.** "Stake is skin in the game." That restates the plutocracy claim; the objection is whose skin. Also a trap: "we would cap stake per identity." The cap reintroduces identity, which slide 12 says stake was meant to replace ("Creating another name would not create another unit of stake"): a capped stake is one-person-one-vote with extra steps, and the fake-identity objection returns. And answering "it is not a token" without saying what it is.

## 4. The arXiv round is a rigged majority, and the lazy 0.50 juror wins it

**From the floor.** "You secretly told 55% of us to type 0.10 and then showed that the mean moved. That is arithmetic, not evidence. Your own banner will say the bloc took the pot. And the person who ignored the evidence and wrote 0.50 for everything just doubled their stake in that round. What did this demonstrate about honest reading?"

**Angle.** Methodological (the demo as evidence).

**Target.** Slide 10, "A bloc can move the room's mean ... This illustration shows what a bloc that size can do to a mean"; facilitator guide, 0:45, "about half the jurors had a private instruction to score 0.10" and "the arXiv round's banner is the data point worth keeping"; the bloc instruction on the phone, "If enough of you comply the centre moves toward the bloc and the honest jurors forfeit to you."

**Why it bites.** `BLOC_FRACTION = 0.55`. With 17 of 30 at 0.10 and 13 honest jurors around 0.85, the ledger gives centre 0.425, band [0.019, 0.831]: all 17 bloc members inside, taking 92.9% of the pot (+27% of stake each); 8 of 13 honest jurors forfeit. arXiv's "credible consensus" is 0.425, "relied on in several fields", when the note says "The honest read is high." The only thing demonstrated is that a majority wins, which is true of every aggregation rule ever proposed. Worse for the thesis: add one juror who ignores the evidence and writes 0.50, and that juror lands 0.21 bands from the centre and takes 18.7% of the pot (+101% of stake) while each honest juror loses 11%. In the round the guide calls "the data point worth keeping", the best-paid strategy is not reading. The forfeit defence cannot fire at the chosen size because the bloc also stretches the yardstick: at 40% (12 of 30) the bloc moves the centre by 0.30 and forfeits 1% of stake; the deck's own slide-10 widget (base jury 0.33 to 0.52, bloc at 0.90) shows the cliff: 20% bloc forfeits everything, 33% forfeits 36%, 40% forfeits 2%, 47% is inside and paid. The materials promise forfeits ("honest jurors forfeit to you" is the bloc's promise, "forfeits" is the handout's defence) and the arithmetic delivers them only below about a third. A small bloc that accepts losing everything can still hurt: 5 of 30 at 0.10 forfeit 100% and drag the band so that 7 of the 25 honest high scorers forfeit too.

**Strongest form.** A demonstration must be able to fail. Experimental economics requires induced value (Smith 1976) and real incentives (Camerer and Hogarth 1999, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty) before an incentive claim is tested; slide 7 says "The points are for the workshop; they are not money." An instructed bloc is a manipulation check, not a treatment; without a control round (open scoring, or a deliberating panel on the same docket) the room cannot tell what sealing bought. The proposed remedy, random draws, does not dilute a bloc that is a proportion of the stake pool: a random sample of a pool that is 55% bloc is 55% bloc in expectation. Only appeals change that, and they are "not performed" (slide 10).

**Trap.** "It is only an illustration." Then the guide's "data point worth keeping" is not data, and slide 10 has no evidence under it. Also a trap: "the bloc paid for it." At 55% it is paid; at 40% it pays 1%. And "the random draw dilutes a bloc": it does not change a proportion.

## 5. Scores are public by name after the reveal, so the buyer never needs the salt

**From the floor.** "Why would a vote buyer want my salt words? After the reveal my name and my score are on every phone in the room. The buyer says: anyone who reveals 0.10 gets paid afterwards. No proof, no bounty, no risk. Your whole vote-buying slide defends against a buyer who is impatient."

**Angle.** Crypto scepticism and mechanism design.

**Target.** Slide 9, "A proof shown to a buyer can be reported ... That makes verification risky for a buyer. It does not prove that every possible bribery arrangement fails"; handout, "Vote buying: A proof shown to a buyer can be reported for a bounty, making verification risky"; the phone, "Opening your sealed score to anyone before the jury opens together is pre-revelation."

**Why it bites.** The results screen prints every juror's name and score (`pname(x.pid)`, `x.score.toFixed(2)`), sorted by score, and the final screen says "Every balance here was derived from the sealed scores, the reveals and the reports, by every phone in the room." The leak bounty protects a buyer's *pre-reveal* verification only; the bloc instruction itself tells the bloc that "it proves nothing anyway: either of you can replace a seal until the scoring closes." A buyer who pays after the reveal, conditional on the public number, needs no proof and runs no risk. The hedge "does not prove that every possible bribery arrangement fails" is doing all the work: the arrangement that fails to be covered is the obvious one.

**Strongest form.** This is Buterin's P + ε attack (Ethereum blog, January 2015) against SchellingCoin-style mechanisms: the attacker promises each voter P + ε, payable only if the attacker's answer *loses*; if everyone expects the others to accept, the answer wins and the attacker pays nothing. It requires post-hoc attributable votes, which this design provides. Here it is even cheaper: with a 0 to 1 scale and a band, the promise "I cover your forfeit plus ε if the centre does not move" makes 0.10 weakly dominant, and at 55% nobody forfeits (attack 4), so the briber's expected cost is zero. The literature's answer is MACI (Buterin, ethresear.ch, 2019, "Minimal anti-collusion infrastructure"): individual votes must be unprovable *after* the tally too, with key-change so a voter cannot prove to a briber what they did. Commit-reveal with public reveals is the configuration MACI was written to replace. Random draws do not help a standing public offer; the briber does not need to know who was drawn.

**Trap.** "The buyer cannot verify before the reveal." Irrelevant; the buyer waits. Also a trap: "the buyer has to pay a lot of people." In the P + ε equilibrium the buyer pays nobody. And "the random draw means the buyer does not know whom to bribe": a public standing offer needs no names.

## 6. The prompt funds the already-funded: reliance is not need, and "who funds it" is excluded

**From the floor.** "Under your prompt the top nominee is Linux, which has the Linux Foundation and every cloud vendor behind it, and PDF, which is Adobe's. You told us to keep 'who runs or funds it' separate. A fund for the research commons needs to know what breaks without *money*, not what breaks without the thing. Who wrote this prompt, and who picked the six names?"

**Angle.** Political and legitimacy.

**Target.** Policy `separate` list: "Quality of its contents", "Who runs or funds it", "How much you use it", "Your own field or taste"; the prompt, "How much does this nominee's completed and maintained work underpin how research is done, shared and kept, now?"; slide 13, "A research community sets a policy; a jury scores the work against it"; handout, "a community sets the policy; a jury supplies scores to inform its decisions."

**Why it bites.** The session is titled "A jury for funding the research commons", yet the prompt asks about current reliance ("now") and excludes governance, funding and openness. Reliance correlates with existing funding, so the ranking the jury produces is close to the inverse of where marginal money matters. The scope line says "open infrastructure", but the prompt does not: openness is enforced by the docket, which the host picks ("leave the six nominees of the docket ticked", "the docket cannot change once summoned"), and the deck says only "A public policy, a docket, a room and an hour" without saying who makes the docket or the policy. In the room, both were made by the presenter. "A community sets the policy" has no mechanism behind it anywhere in the materials; the only concrete authority in the design is the host (who can also remove seats: "Remove a duplicate or stray seat with the cross on its chip").

**Strongest form.** Eghbal, "Roads and Bridges" (Ford Foundation, 2016): the critical digital infrastructure that needs funding is the under-maintained, invisible layer, not the famous layer; a reliance score is a fame score. For a funder, the decision-relevant quantity is counterfactual impact of the grant, not the counterfactual of the artefact's disappearance. Ostrom again: who writes the rules is the commons question; a mechanism that treats the policy and the docket as exogenous has put the only political decisions outside itself and then claimed to be apolitical. Slide 11 concedes "A clear policy can still encode a disputed value judgment: put that argument in the public document", but no public document, process or author is named.

**Trap.** "The policy is just an example; a community would write its own." Then the demo shows nothing about the design's output, which is whatever the prompt-writer makes it. Also a trap: "the docket filters for open infrastructure." That concedes the docket-setter has the power the jury appears to have. And "we excluded funding to avoid bias": for a commons funder, that bias is the question.

## 7. No reasons, bounties for reporting colleagues, fines for talking

**From the floor.** "Open science is reasons in public: signed reviews, registered reports, open evaluation. Your design produces a number with no reasons, and your bloc's cover story works precisely because, as your own screen says, 'nobody can see why you scored, only what you scored'. On top of that you fine me for telling a colleague what I think before the reveal and pay the colleague for reporting me. That is not a jury; it is an informant scheme."

**Angle.** Open-science norms.

**Target.** The bloc instruction, "nobody can see why you scored, only what you scored"; the phone's "Anti-pre-revelation" panel, "Opening your sealed score to anyone before the jury opens together is pre-revelation. Whoever you show can turn it in"; slide 9, "A report fines the leaker; the reporter receives part of that penalty"; the guide, "Anyone who reads another juror's proof can type it into 'Report' and take a bounty from the juror who showed it."

**Why it bites.** Slide 2 attacks committees because "everyone else takes their word for it", but the jury's output is equally unauditable: a number per juror, no justification, and the final screen's "derived ... with the same arithmetic" verifies the sum of unverifiable inputs. The design removes the one artefact a funder can act on and defend in public, the written reason, and replaces it with a reputational exposure (your name next to a bare number on thirty phones). The leak mechanism makes ordinary scholarly behaviour, discussing a judgment with a colleague, a fineable offence with a bounty, and by default the proof is shown on the juror's own screen ("Proofs: by default each juror's proof ... is on their own screen"), so a neighbour reading over a shoulder can collect.

**Strongest form.** The open peer review literature (van Rooyen et al. 1999, BMJ; Bravo et al. 2019, Nature Communications) finds that the value and the cost of openness live in the *report*, not the score; a signed number without a report has the costs of open identity and none of the benefits. Funders are accountable for public money; a committee minute can be contested, a stake-weighted mean cannot. Delphi (attack 13) and citizens' assemblies (Fishkin 2009; OECD 2020) derive their legitimacy from documented reasoning; this design derives its legitimacy from a hash.

**Trap.** "The reasons are in the policy." The policy is the question; the juror's reason is the answer, and it is discarded. Also a trap: "jurors can publish reasons afterwards." Then reasons can be coordinated (attack 18) and nothing in the design reads them. And "the bounty only targets vote-sellers": the text says "Whoever you show can turn it in", with no exception for colleagues.

## 8. The "consensus" for PDF or LaTeX is a number nobody in the room gave

**From the floor.** "Half the room thinks PDF is 0.85 and the rest think 0.45. Your consensus is 0.69. Not one juror said 0.69. A funder who acts on 0.69 is acting on a reading nobody holds. And for LaTeX, the twelve people who actually use it are paid nothing while the eighteen who never opened it take ninety percent of the pot."

**Angle.** Epistemic and methodological.

**Target.** Slide 3, "One policy → independent scores → a jury's centre" and "A credible consensus is a candidate answer they can act on"; PDF note, "the band is set by the larger group, and the smaller one pays for the split"; LaTeX note, "Your field is not the field."

**Why it bites.** With 18 at about 0.85 and 12 at about 0.45 the ledger gives centre 0.695: the "credible consensus" is a point in the gap between two readings the policy itself says are both "inside the policy". With 16 against 14 the centre is 0.668 and almost nobody forfeits, so the mechanism reports agreement where there is a clean disagreement. For LaTeX with 12 users at about 0.85 and 18 non-users at about 0.35: centre 0.553, 5 of the 12 users forfeit, the user group nets zero, the non-user group takes 90% of the pot. The mechanism pays the people with the least contact with the nominee. A mean of a bimodal distribution is not a consensus; it is an artefact, and the band hides the bimodality by widening around it.

**Strongest form.** Any aggregation text: a location statistic is only meaningful for a unimodal distribution; the honest output of a split jury is "the jury is split, here is why", which is what a Delphi round or a panel minute produces. Arrow's theorem is overkill, but the audience's intuition is the same: there is no neutral way to turn two incompatible readings into one number, and the mechanism's choice (stake-weighted mean) is a political choice presented as arithmetic.

**Trap.** "The mean is the standard aggregate." Not for a split; show the split. Also a trap: "the spread is reported too." The funder gets the centre: slide 3's arrow ends at "a jury's centre".

## 9. Recusal is free, scoring is risky: the honest-but-unsure leave

**From the floor.** "Recusing costs nothing. Scoring can cost my whole stake. If I am honest about not being sure, I recuse. Who is left scoring? The confident and the interested. You have built a filter for overconfidence."

**Angle.** Epistemic and operational.

**Target.** Slide 7, "Recuse yourself if you cannot apply the policy"; slide 11, "Recusal lets a juror step out rather than invent a score"; the phone, "Recused ... No stake, no share, no fine for silence"; slide 6, "jurors who know the field or recuse. The objections that follow test each one."

**Why it bites.** The payoff is asymmetric: inside the band a juror shares `ROUND_REWARD` (4 antes) with everybody else inside, so a typical gain is a fraction of an ante; outside, the loss runs to a whole ante. Recusal is exactly zero. For anyone uncertain where the room is (which, given attack 1, is anyone honest), recusal dominates. Meanwhile a seated juror who fails to seal loses the whole ante ("did not reveal in time: −2520 each"), so the safe move on every nominee is to recuse first. The materials present recusal as integrity; the arithmetic makes it the risk-free default and selects the scoring jury for confidence and for having something at stake in the answer. In the proposed design, stakers who paid for the seat will not recuse; the drawn juror who does not know the field will guess the room instead (attack 1). No objection slide tests this condition: slides 8 to 12 cover herding, vote buying, collusion, a vague policy and fake identities; "jurors who know the field or recuse" is asserted on slide 6 and never examined, contrary to "The objections that follow test each one."

**Strongest form.** Self-selection in surveys and in prediction: those who opt in are not those who know (Burgman et al. 2011 again; the overconfidence literature from Kruger and Dunning 1999 onward). In structured expert judgment the fix is to elicit confidence intervals and weight by calibration, not to offer a free exit.

**Trap.** "Recusal is the honest choice." Free exit selects for the confident. Also a trap: "in the full design stake makes recusal costly." Then the uninformed guess.

## 10. Six rounds with names and balances on every phone: by round three jurors forecast the room

**From the floor.** "After every nominee my phone shows everyone's name, score and balance, and the facilitator tells us which end of the scale was right. By the third round I know exactly how generous this room is and who the outliers are. You sealed each round and unsealed the room."

**Angle.** Methodological.

**Target.** Slide 8, "Seal first; open together. The centre is hidden while scores can still change, so jurors cannot copy an unseen score"; slide 6, "a visible first score replaces fifteen readings with one"; the guide, "Walk the room through its own results: the mean, the band, who was paid, who forfeited, the note under the table."

**Why it bites.** Within a round the defence holds. Across rounds it does not: the results table lists every juror by name sorted by score; the seat chips show every juror's running balance during the next round's scoring; and the note shown on every phone after grading tells the room the intended answer ("The top of the scale", "The bottom of the scale", "The honest read is high"). Before round one, the facilitator reads worked examples at 0.90, 0.55 and 0.10 aloud, and the Inkscape note says Inkscape is "the same shape as the worked colour map": the presenter is the visible first score. The tight Linux band the deck will point to is manufactured by calibration between rounds, by the presenter, not by the prompt.

**Strongest form.** Information cascades do not need simultaneity; a sequence of public outcomes with feedback is the textbook setting (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch 1992; Banerjee 1992). Lorenz et al. (2011) is directly on point: after a few rounds of seeing the group's aggregate, estimates converge and confidence rises while accuracy does not. In a repeated game with public histories, the equilibrium is to play the room's prior.

**Trap.** "Each nominee is a fresh seal." The room's generosity and each juror's position are public after every round. Also a trap: "names are shown so the arithmetic is auditable." True, and that is the leak.

## 11. Nobody knows "research as a whole"; the anchors are a head count of fields

**From the floor.** "Your prompt asks how much a nominee underpins research as a whole. Nobody in this room, or anywhere, knows that. And your anchors are a count of fields: 'matters to one field' is 0.25. A tool one whole discipline cannot work without can never score above a quarter. That is not a neutral scale, it is a vote for big fields."

**Angle.** Epistemic and legitimacy.

**Target.** Slide 5, the anchors "0.25 matters to one field / 0.50 relied on in several fields / 0.75 widely relied on / 1.00 research stops without it"; the tests "how many rely"; slide 6, "a prompt concrete enough to read alike"; the LaTeX note, "The prompt asks about research as a whole, so your own field gets no extra weight."

**Why it bites.** Condition 2 of the design argument ("jurors who know the field") is unsatisfiable for this prompt: no juror knows research as a whole, every juror extrapolates from their own field, and the LaTeX and NumPy notes say the result is the field mix of the room. The anchors then encode a majoritarian value judgment, breadth over criticality, which slide 11 would call "a disputed value judgment" to be argued "in the public document"; there is no such document, and the argument was settled by the author. The "concrete" prompt is concrete about the wrong thing: it is precise enough to produce a tight band and vague enough that the band is about the room.

**Strongest form.** Measurement validity: a scale whose anchors are counts of adopting fields measures diffusion, not infrastructure criticality; the two are known to diverge (Eghbal 2016; the whole "invisible infrastructure" argument). For the humanities and small-field infrastructure in the room (digital editions, corpus tools, a single observatory's pipeline), the scale is structurally hostile.

**Trap.** "That is what 'research as a whole' means." Then nobody is competent and condition 2 fails by construction. Also a trap: "small fields can write their own policy." Then the commons fragments into field-sized juries, and the "strangers" framing is gone.

## 12. Output-agreement mechanisms have uninformative equilibria; the known fixes reward the opposite

**From the floor.** "Paying people for agreeing with a hidden peer is called output agreement in the peer-prediction literature, and the theorem there is that it has an equilibrium where everyone reports the obvious answer regardless of what they know, and that equilibrium pays more than truth-telling. The mechanisms that fix this, Bayesian truth serum and surprisingly popular, pay for the answer that is more common than expected. You are paying for the answer that is most expected."

**Angle.** Methodological, with a theoretical edge.

**Target.** Slide 6, "It is a design argument, not a proof"; handout, "It does not prove that honest scoring always wins."

**Why it bites.** The hedge admits there is no proof; it does not acknowledge that there is a counter-result. The game is an output-agreement mechanism on a continuous scale (the ESP game of von Ahn and Dabbish 2004 is the ancestor): payment depends only on closeness to peers' reports.

**Strongest form.** Miller, Resnick and Zeckhauser (2005, Management Science, "Eliciting informative feedback: the peer-prediction method") show truthful reporting is an equilibrium only under a known common prior and that other equilibria exist; Jurca and Faltings (2009, JAIR) show the uninformative equilibria can Pareto-dominate the truthful one; Dasgupta and Ghosh (2013, WWW) show output agreement incentivises the *most likely* answer rather than the observed one; Waggoner and Chen (2014, HCOMP, "Output agreement mechanisms and common knowledge") give the condition under which output agreement is truthful: each agent's signal must be the best predictor of others' signals, which is precisely what fails when a famous nominee has a known popular answer. Prelec (2004, Science, "A Bayesian truth serum for subjective data") and Prelec, Seo and McCoy (2017, Nature) are the published mechanisms for exactly this problem, subjective judgments with no ground truth, and they reward the surprisingly common answer, the opposite incentive from closeness to the mean. The deck's central sentence, "your best forecast of where careful strangers land is your own careful reading", is the output-agreement truthfulness condition stated as if it were a conclusion.

**Trap.** "It is a design argument, not a proof." The literature has the counter-proof; a hedge does not neutralise a theorem. Also a trap: "truth serum is too complicated for a room." That concedes the mechanism was chosen for simplicity over truthfulness, which is a fair thing to say out loud but not a defence of slide 6.

## 13. Delphi, IDEA and Cooke already do this with reasons, iteration and calibration

**From the floor.** "Anonymous independent estimates, feedback, convergence: that is the Delphi method, sixty years old, used for every reporting guideline in my field. Delphi feeds back the distribution *and the reasons* and iterates; you feed back a forfeit. What does the forfeit add that the second round of a Delphi does not, except that nobody learns anything?"

**Angle.** Comparative.

**Target.** Slide 2, "A judgment needs people and a shared standard"; slide 6, "Independent readings carry information."

**Why it bites.** The materials never mention structured expert judgment, the established practice for "a judgment, no ground truth" (slide 2), in a room full of people who run Delphi panels. The coherence game is the first round of a Delphi with a penalty attached and the reasons deleted.

**Strongest form.** Dalkey and Helmer (1963, Management Science); Rowe and Wright (1999, International Journal of Forecasting) on when Delphi improves judgment (feedback of reasons, not just statistics); the IDEA protocol (Hemming et al. 2018, Methods in Ecology and Evolution: Investigate, Discuss, Estimate, Aggregate), whose discussion step measurably improves estimates and whose first-round-only variant is what this game plays; Cooke's classical model (1991), which weights by calibration on seed questions and has decades of validation data (Cooke and Goossens 2008). All three keep the minority view in the record. None pays for conformity.

**Trap.** "Delphi takes weeks." The hour is bought by discarding the reasons, which is the thing this audience values. Also a trap: "Delphi herds." Delphi's first round is independent, and its feedback is of reasons; the coherence game feeds back names, scores and forfeits (attack 10).

## 14. Funders already answered "numbers nobody can verify" with lotteries

**From the floor.** "The Swiss National Science Foundation, the Health Research Council of New Zealand and the Volkswagen Foundation already concluded that review scores above a threshold are noise and draw lots. That solves your slide-2 problem by admitting it. Why add a stake-weighted game in front of a decision that a coin solves more honestly?"

**Angle.** Comparative.

**Target.** Slide 2, "Every funding round ends in a split that needs numbers nobody can verify"; slide 13, "a jury supplies scores to inform allocation."

**Why it bites.** The deck motivates the design with the unreliability of committee numbers and never mentions the response the funding field has actually adopted. The proposed design still produces a ranking number per nominee and asks a committee to act on it (slide 13), reproducing the thing slide 2 complains about.

**Strongest form.** Cole, Cole and Simon (1981, Science, "Chance and consensus in peer review"); Pier et al. (2018, PNAS, low agreement among NIH reviewers); Bornmann, Mutz and Daniel (2010, PLOS ONE) meta-analysis of inter-rater reliability; Fang and Casadevall (2016, mBio, "the case for a modified lottery"); Avin (2018) on random allocation; the SNSF's lottery for ties (2021) and the HRC Explorer Grants lottery (Liu et al. 2020, Research Integrity and Peer Review). A lottery above a threshold has no Keynesian beauty contest, no plutocracy, no vote buying and no informants, and its legitimacy has been studied in the field rather than in a room.

**Trap.** "Lotteries ignore merit." They are used above a merit threshold; a jury could set the threshold, which is a far smaller claim than "credible consensus" and would need a different slide 3.

## 15. This is Kleros applied to importance, with Kleros's record

**From the floor.** "Commit-reveal votes, coherent jurors paid from the stakes of incoherent ones, random draws weighted by stake, appeals to larger juries: that is Kleros. Kleros has been live since 2018. Its known problems are rational ignorance, jurors voting the expected majority rather than the case, small-court capture, and appeal fees that favour the richer party. What in your design is different, other than the question?"

**Angle.** Crypto scepticism.

**Target.** Slide 10, "The full design proposes random draws weighted by stake, forfeits and appeals to larger juries"; slide 12.

**Why it bites.** The materials present the full design's components as proposals without naming their origin or record. A crypto-literate participant will name it within a minute, and the rest of the room will hear "this has been tried, by a crypto court."

**Strongest form.** Lesaege, Ast and George, Kleros whitepaper (2019): coherence-based redistribution of stakes, stake-weighted sortition, appeals with escalating fees. Documented issues: the "vote with the majority" heuristic (the Schelling game paying for predicted majorities, which is attack 1 observed in the wild), low juror effort, bribery discussions leading to proposals for hidden votes, and the cost asymmetry of appeals. Also the SchellingCoin origin (Buterin 2014) and its P + ε vulnerability (attack 5).

**Trap.** "Ours is different because scores are sealed." Kleros is commit-reveal too. Also a trap: "Kleros works." Then cite its record, because the sceptic will.

## 16. Hashes and salt words on the presenter's own server: crypto theatre

**From the floor.** "The room is a Cloudflare Worker you run. I have to trust your server for the roster, the clock, the close, and the reveal. The hash only stops you from reading my score early, and I trust you not to anyway. So what is the sealing for, apart from the two Bitcoin seed words I have to keep secret from my neighbour?"

**Angle.** Crypto scepticism and practical.

**Target.** Slide 4, "No account. No wallet."; slide 7, "Keep your two salt words private"; slide 9, "commit / reveal"; the phone, "Nobody can see your number, including the host."

**Why it bites.** `ARCHITECTURE.md`: "A room code in the URL is the only admission control; anyone with a seat may summon, take over, end, start a new game or wipe." The timestamps, the seated list and the cutoff are all the Room's. The commitment protects jurors from the host only, and the host is the person asking them to trust the design. The salt words are the BIP-39 Bitcoin mnemonic list (`SALT_WORDS` begins "abandon ability able about above absent absorb abstract absurd abuse access accident account", and includes "satoshi"), which a sceptic will spot and tweet. The working paper is titled "Decentralized Curation". The "No wallet" line reads as evasion once the proposed design's stake, forfeits and random draws are on the table (slides 10 and 12) with no statement of what stake is. For this audience the honest comparison is a sealed Google Form with a closing time: same herding protection, no words to keep secret.

**Strongest form.** The trust argument is standard: a commitment scheme only buys you something against a party you do not otherwise trust; here the only untrusted party is the host, who controls everything else. The cultural argument: the audience's scepticism is about tokens replacing judgment; borrowing the vocabulary (stake, forfeit, salt, seal, anti-pre-revelation, bounty) without the substance earns the suspicion without the benefit.

**Trap.** "It is not crypto." The paper's title, the stake-weighted design and the BIP-39 words say otherwise; better to own it. Also a trap: "the hashes let you verify." Verify what: that the host did not alter a score on the host's server.

## 17. The demo cannot fail: either result confirms the thesis

**From the floor.** "If the room agrees you will call it coherence. If it disagrees you will call the policy vague. If the bloc wins you will say that is what a bloc does. What result today would have counted against the design?"

**Angle.** Methodological.

**Target.** Slide 3, "We examine what the incentives reward and where agreement can still go wrong"; slide 11, "Make the policy concrete enough to read together"; the guide, "Note what the room said at each objection; the arXiv round's banner is the data point worth keeping."

**Why it bites.** The nominees are six of the most famous names in research computing, with blurbs written by the presenter that lean on the intended answer (Linux "runs every machine on the TOP500", NumPy "behind the first image of a black hole", arXiv "more than three million papers"). Sixty seconds per nominee (`COMMIT_DURATION_S = 60`) with a two-sentence blurb as "the evidence": slide 6's "careful reading of the evidence" is a minute's reading of the presenter's sentence. Points, not money. No control condition. The notes after each round grade the room against the author's expectation ("The honest read is high"). A thirty-person room produced by a conference theme is the sample. Nothing in the run of show is a prediction that could be wrong.

**Strongest form.** Popper's risky prediction; or, more concretely for this audience, the registered-report standard they advocate for everyone else: state the outcome that would falsify the claim before running it. Camerer and Hogarth (1999) on incentives; Harrison and Rutström (2008) on hypothetical bias. The Playwright run with thirty browsers (`README.md`) tests the software, not the claim.

**Trap.** "The room will see for itself." The room will see whatever it does, and both outcomes are pre-explained. Also a trap: offering the end-to-end test as evidence.

## 18. Project communities will stake to sit on their own juries; no conflict check is possible

**From the floor.** "In the full design, who stakes to be on the jury that scores NumPy? NumPy's users and maintainers. Your condition is 'no coordination outside the game', but they do not need to coordinate; they share a view. Peer review handles this with declared conflicts and exclusion. Your jurors are anonymous until the reveal, give no reasons, and recuse only if they feel like it."

**Angle.** Political.

**Target.** Slide 6, "no coordination outside the game"; slide 8, "it does not prevent coordination outside the game"; slide 12, stake-weighted draws; the policy's "How much you use it" exclusion.

**Why it bites.** The materials treat coordination as something that must be arranged. In a small field it is ambient: same labs, same lists, same Slack, same conference. The design cannot see a conflict of interest because it does not read reasons (attack 7) and recusal is voluntary (attack 9). The only remedy named is the stake-weighted random draw, which draws from the people who stake, who are the interested parties. The policy's instruction to keep "How much you use it" separate cannot be enforced on a sealed number.

**Strongest form.** Conflict-of-interest policy in grant review (NIH, ERC, UKRI) exists because declared interests are the cheapest reliable defence; the quadratic-funding literature (Buterin, Hitzig and Weyl 2019; Gitcoin's rounds) found collusion among aligned small holders to be the dominant attack, requiring identity (Gitcoin Passport) and cluster-matching (Miller, Weyl and Erichsen 2022) fixes, both of which reintroduce the identity layer slide 12 says stake replaces.

**Trap.** "The policy says to exclude how much you use it." Unenforceable on a sealed number. Also a trap: "random draws dilute them." Project communities are large stakers.

## 19. Appeals to larger juries cannot fix a shared bias

**From the floor.** "If the first jury was wrong because the room shares a view, the larger jury shares it too. Appeals fix sampling error, not bias. And who pays for the appeal? The side with more money."

**Angle.** Epistemic and political.

**Target.** Slide 10, "appeals to larger juries"; handout, "Collusion: Random draws weighted by stake, forfeits and appeals to larger juries are proposed."

**Why it bites.** Appeals are the only defence offered against a bloc above a third of the stake (attack 4) and against a shared bias (attack 1), and they are "not performed" and not described: no trigger, no cost, no who-decides. If the bloc is a shared belief (the arXiv cover story is one), the larger jury has it in the same proportion.

**Strongest form.** Condorcet with correlated votes (Ladha 1992; Berg 1993): enlarging a jury whose errors are correlated does not converge to truth. Kleros's appeal fee structure (attack 15) is the live example of appeals favouring the richer litigant.

**Trap.** "A larger jury is more accurate." Only if errors are independent, which is the condition the whole report says fails.

## 20. A sleeping phone or a lost salt is a total loss

**From the floor.** "My phone locked during the reveal and I lost my stake. In the real design that is money. You are making a researcher's income depend on iOS auto-lock and on two words stored in a browser's local storage."

**Angle.** Practical.

**Target.** Guide, "A juror whose phone slept through the reveal is counted as not revealing for that nominee"; the phone, "did not reveal in time: −2520 each" and "Salt missing: The salt of your sealed score is not on this device, so it cannot be opened here. The jury counts you as not revealing"; `ARCHITECTURE.md`, "Nobody else holds a salt."

**Why it bites.** Absence costs a whole ante while recusal costs nothing, so a device failure is punished harder than any dishonest score inside the band. In a room of thirty phones on conference Wi-Fi this will happen live, and the guide's answer is "Say so before the first round so nobody is surprised." Liveness as a condition of keeping your money is a known weakness of commit-reveal schemes and a griefing vector (jam the reveal window).

**Strongest form.** Any commit-reveal deployment post-mortem; the general point that a mechanism whose forfeit falls on the unlucky as hard as on the dishonest is unfair in a way an audience of practitioners will feel immediately.

**Trap.** "It is a workshop." The full design is money. Also a trap: "we warn them first." Consent does not make the rule good.

## 21. What does 0.43 for arXiv mean in euros, what does the design cost, and the committee still decides

**From the floor.** "I run a fund. You give me 0.43 for arXiv and 0.92 for Linux. How much money is that? Who put up the reward pool and the stakes? And slide 13 says deciding whether to fund 'remains a choice for the community', so I still need my committee. You have added a game in front of it and called it a solution to committees."

**Angle.** Legitimacy and practical.

**Target.** Slide 2, "Committees produce these numbers, and everyone else takes their word for it"; slide 13, "What it costs: a public policy, a docket, a room and an hour" and "Deciding whether to fund from those scores remains a choice for the community."

**Why it bites.** The deck attacks committees in slide 2 and reinstates them in slide 13. The cost line counts the workshop's cost, not the design's: a reward pool, stakes (whose?), a stake registry, dispute handling and the infrastructure, none of which are priced or sourced. The output is a reliance score with no mapping to allocation, no budget constraint and no comparison across nominees except by rank.

**Strongest form.** Any funder's practical question: the decision-relevant number is marginal impact per euro under a budget; a 0 to 1 reliance score, even if perfect, does not provide it. Hanson's futarchy and prediction-market proposals at least resolve against a measurable outcome; the replication markets (Dreber et al. 2015, PNAS; Camerer et al. 2018, Nature Human Behaviour) worked because replication is observable. Importance is not, and the deck says so on slide 2 ("no oracle exists"). A market or jury with no resolution is a beauty contest by construction.

**Trap.** "The community decides." Then nothing is settled by the mechanism, and slide 2's problem stands exactly as stated.

## 22. The deck, handout, guide and game contradict each other

**From the floor.** "Your handout lists four objections, your deck five. Your slide says the objections test all three conditions; none tests the second. Your bloc instruction promises that the honest jurors forfeit to the bloc; your own results banner has a branch for the bloc moving the centre 'without costing anyone a forfeit'. Your slide says 'credible consensus'; your phone says 'Graded against the jury, not against the truth'. Which of these do you mean?"

**Angle.** Methodological.

**Target and why it bites.**

- Handout "Four objections" (Herding, Vote buying, Collusion, Vague policy) versus the deck's five (slides 8 to 12 add "fake identities"). The one dropped is the one that introduces stake, which is the most contested idea in the room.
- Slide 6: "This rests on three things ... The objections that follow test each one." Slides 8 to 12 test the prompt (s11) and coordination (s8, s9, s10) and add identity (s12). Nothing tests "jurors who know the field or recuse".
- Bloc instruction: "If enough of you comply the centre moves toward the bloc and the honest jurors forfeit to you." `blocBanner` has an amber branch: "The bloc landed inside the band and took X% of the pot. It moved the centre without costing anyone a forfeit." At exactly half the room that is the branch that fires; at 55% the honest do forfeit, but only because the bloc is a majority.
- Guide: "about half the jurors had a private instruction"; code: `BLOC_FRACTION = 0.55`. Slide 10: "This illustration shows what a bloc that size can do to a mean", but the widget's bloc scores 0.90 against a low base jury, the inverse of the live round, and the widget's own cliff (forfeit at 33%, free at 47%) is never stated in words.
- Slide 3, "A credible consensus"; handout, "A credible consensus is a candidate answer that a community can act on"; the phone, "Graded against the jury, not against the truth." The phone is honest; the slides are not consistent with it.
- Guide title "How to run the ninety-minute session" for a slot the same document says is eighty minutes.
- Slide 3, "This workshop starts from completed work"; the prompt, "completed and maintained work"; infrastructure is never completed, which is the funding problem.
- Slide 6 and slide 8 speak of "fifteen" readings for a thirty-person room; trivial, but a meta-scientist will notice the figures are illustrations rather than the room's data.

**Trap.** "Typos." The bloc-instruction promise, the missing fifth objection and the untested second condition are substantive.
