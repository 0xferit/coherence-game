06 / How you are paid

The jury sets the centre, and the yardstick.

The centre is the mean weighted by stake. The band follows the jury’s own spread. Inside it, reward shares grow with closeness to the centre; outside it, forfeits grow with distance. The game pays for coherence with the room. Whether that is enough is what we are here to examine.

Why hidden, and why honest

Sealed scores leave jurors only two things in common: the prompt and the evidence. Independent readings carry information; a visible first score replaces fifteen readings with one.

You are paid for landing near a centre you cannot see: a Keynesian beauty contest. Two things answer Keynes. Nothing to copy, so no early score can become the thing to match, and your best forecast of where careful strangers land is your own careful reading of the evidence. And a written prompt, which Keynes’s contestants never had: the Schelling point, the answer strangers converge on without talking, because each expects the others to read it the same way.

This rests on three things: a prompt concrete enough to read alike, jurors who know the field or recuse, and no coordination outside the game. The objections that follow test the first and the third; the docket tests the second, each time a nominee you do not know comes up. It is a design argument, not a proof.

    08 / Objection: herding

    Agreement can be an echo.

    Seal first; open together.

    The centre is hidden while scores can still change, so jurors cannot copy an unseen score. This limits visible herding; it does not prevent coordination outside the game.

    09 / Objection: vote buying

    A proof shown to a buyer can be reported.

    A juror can prove their sealed score by showing the score and salt words.

    Whoever receives that proof can report it for a bounty. That makes verification risky for a buyer. It does not prove that every possible bribery arrangement fails.

    Do not show your salt words before the round is graded. A report fines the leaker; the reporter receives part of that penalty.

    10 / Objection: collusion

    A bloc can move the room’s mean.

    The arXiv round gave a bloc an instruction. This illustration shows what a bloc that size can do to a mean. The full design proposes random draws weighted by stake, forfeits and appeals to larger juries. The working paper establishes no safe collusion threshold. Random draws are discussed here; we do not perform one live. In this room’s arithmetic the forfeit bites a bloc below about a third of the seats; above that the bloc is paid, so the arXiv round uses a majority on purpose, to show the attack rather than the defence.

    11 / Objection: a vague policy

    Make the policy concrete enough to read together.

    Worked anchors give strangers something more useful than “importance”.

    Recusal lets a juror step out rather than invent a score. A clear policy can still encode a disputed value judgment: put that argument in the public document.

    12 / Objection: fake identities

    In the proposed design, stake weighs seats.

    Creating another name would not create another unit of stake.

    The full design weights the draw by stake rather than by head. Concentrated stake remains a concern. This workshop uses equal seats and points, so it does not demonstrate that defence.
